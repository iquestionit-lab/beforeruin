How This Was Built  ·  And What Was Rejected
The Workbench

This series asks you to check what you were handed instead of taking it on somebody's word. It would be a poor thing to ask that and then hand you a tool you cannot look inside.

Why this page exists

Every decision that shaped Scripnotes and this site is written down here, with the reasoning, and with the options that were thrown out. The thrown-out ones matter more than the kept ones. Anybody can publish what they decided. Publishing what you rejected is how a reader finds out whether you thought about it at all.

Some of these entries record mistakes. Those stay up. A shop that only records its good days is not keeping a record, it is keeping an advertisement.

The record

What Was Decided, And Why

12 October 2026

A search returns your notes, not an answer

Most search tools return a result. A result claims to be a fact, and a fact invites exactly two responses — believe it, or reject it. Neither of those is reading.

So Scripnotes does not return an answer. You went to the witnesses; the witnesses said what they said; the tool is the clerk at the table writing it down while you stand there. What comes back is a transcript of your own consultation. It carries a date, it names its sources, and it points past itself to the thing it was taken from.

That is also why the notes are yours and not mine. If the tool wrote them and nobody said so, you would reasonably assume they were mine — and a handed-down thing wearing somebody else's name is the exact fault this series exists to expose. It would be a poor joke to build a small working model of it into the tool.

Rejected

"Results." Implies retrieval of settled fact. The whole point is that the fact is not settled and you are the one who gets to look.

Notes presented without naming the clerk. A note with no hand behind it is a rumor. Every page of notes says plainly that the tool assembled them and lists what it assembled them from.

12 October 2026

The notes are free, and will never be behind an email

No account, no login, no address required — not for the notes, not for the sources, not for the search. Not now and not later.

Before Ruin makes one claim: go check me. A wall in front of the evidence does not weaken that claim, it reverses it, because then the only people who can check are the ones who paid a toll. A teacher you cannot check without paying is the arrangement this whole series argues against.

There is a mailing list, and it gets something real — the notes delivered to an inbox, the schedule before it is published, invitations to the live sessions. None of that is the evidence. The evidence stays open. The test for anything else that gets offered: is this the evidence, or is it the errand? Evidence is open. Errands can be delivered.

Rejected

Gating the notes to grow the list. It would have worked, and it would have handed every critic one sentence: he says look it up, and then asks for your email. Said once, that is the only thing anybody remembers.

12 October 2026

The name: Scripnotes

It was called the Recovery Engine. Before that the working name was ScriptSearch, which turned out to belong to somebody else.

"Recovery Engine" named the effect rather than the thing — and a name that describes what something does to you is a name nobody can repeat to a friend. Scripnotes says what it is, and it now agrees with what the tool actually hands you. The thing and its label match, which is rarer than it sounds.

Rejected

"Like having a scholar's library for free." It is not that, and it should not be sold as that. It does one thing a scholar's library does — it takes you under the English — and it does that free, in about ninety seconds, with no password. Claim the narrow thing and it holds your weight. Claim the wide thing and somebody will stand on it and it will give.

12 October 2026

We advertised an address that did not work

For some weeks the end of each broadcast read sources for every claim in this episode: beforeruin.app. That is the home page. It told a viewer where the building was and left them to find the room, and the notes are several screens down.

Underneath that was a second fault nobody had noticed. Even a viewer who typed beforeruin.app/notes would not have reached the notes, because the site had never been configured to resolve addresses without a file extension. Only beforeruin.app/notes.html worked, and nobody types that off a television screen.

Both are fixed. The address on air is now beforeruin.app/notes, and it lands on a page that lists every episode by number and name.

Why this entry is here

It would have been easy to fix this quietly. A record that only holds good decisions is not a record, it is an advertisement — and this series spends its hours arguing that a text you cannot audit is a text somebody has been editing.
